Coauthor Writing Guide - UPC
A paper should allow readers to quickly discover the main
results, and then, if interested, to examine the supporting
evidence. Structure your paper to support this behavior:
1) Describe the work in the context of accepted scientiﬁc
knowledge.
2) State the idea that is being investigated, often as a theory
or hypothesis.
3) Explain what is new about the idea, what is being
evaluated, or what contribution the paper is making.
4) Justify the theory, by proofs or experiments.

Abstract—The teaching of robot movements has always been
considered a complex subject in which there is a lot of interest,
since the slightest change in the robot programming can generate
a high downtime that can last more than a month. In this work,
we carried out a study of human movements to implement
a new method of robot programming by demonstration using
neural networks. Current methods require specialists with high
mathematical and logical knowledge to teach robot movements.
Using a famous pose estimation algorithm called Open Pose and a
3D lifting method we obtain the estimated pose of the person arm
in a simulated 3D space. Then, we use various classiﬁcation tools
to traduce the predicted pose into the robot. The results show
that it is feasible to make robot programming more accessible
using pose estimation.
Index Terms —Pose Estimation, Robot Programming by
Demonstration, Open Pose, Neural Networks.
I. I NTRODUCTION
What is the problem?
  
Currently, many companies use robots with Machine Learn-
ing technology to perform a variety of tasks, such as welding,
machine care, material removal or other activity, since it
would help humans to work together with robots, in order
to enhance the operation of its warehouses and factories.
After the emergence of the pandemic caused by COVID 19
in 2020, multiple companies are re-evaluating their business
model to adapt to this situation, which has accelerated the
introduction of robots in various industries, since this virus
has exposed the limitations of human labor 1. The method
currently used to establish the movements of a robot is
quite comprehensive and has a complex learning curve,
since any alteration in robots would mean weeks or months
of reprogramming by people who require high knowledge,
which generates a robot downtime, a high cost and low
consumption time for the company [1].
.
Why is it interesting and important?
  
Our goal is to provide an application that allows estimating
the pose of a person’s arms in a simulated 3D space to
transfer said information to a robot, which will allow people
who have little or no knowledge in programming and / or
robotics could program the actions to be carried out by a
robot.
.
Why is it hard? (e.g., why do naive approaches fail?)
  
You could estimate the pose of a video with some algorithms
as OpenPose [2], DeepCut [3], RMPE (Alpha Pose) [4] or
PoseNet [5]. However, it isn’t perfect because even though
OpenPose generates better results than the other algorithms
mentioned previously, it needs a 3D lifting method [6] to
convert information obtained in 2D to 3D. Additionally, it
should be considered that information from the human body
can’t be directly translated into a robot, since the latter has
a large number of rotors.
.
Why hasn’t it been solved before? (or, what’s wrong with previous proposed solutions? How does ours differ?)
  
There are some solutions that have a similar approach, since
they seek to do robot programming by demonstration. For
example, in [7], the authors are inspired by the Evolutionary
Algorithms framework to propose a framework, called by
them as EPG. Therefore, they analyze the “policies” to
predict movement through trial and error, rather than using
a pose estimation method. This allows robots to perform
movements learned by human demonstration in the face of
possible changes in the environment. However, this process
is relatively slow, which generates an unnecessary waste of
time in cases where there are no changes in the environment.
In [1], the authors use the YOLO and OpenPose algorithms
to get the action that is performed and smooths the results
with a Gaussian ﬁlter, without using nor estimating human
pose. Instead, we estimate the human pose with OpenPose
algorithm and then use a neural network to help us adapt the
movement so that it can be reproduced by the robot.
.
What are the key components of our approach and results? Also include any speciﬁc limitations.
  
The key components of our research are composed by Open
Pose, the most popular free non-commercial pose estimation
method used to obtain the 2D pose of the person. Then, we
use the 3D lifting method in order to translate the estimated
2D pose to a 3D pose. These method of 3D pose estimation
is fast but has some precision problems with the estimated
points in the same axis of the point of view. Furthermore,
we use the Unity3D to generate a dataset of 3D models and
the ROS integration to simulate a robotic arm that repeats
the human movements.
.
Summary of contributions
  
Our main contributions are as follows:
• We are implementing a method that allows us to trans-
form the input of a video into a 3D arm estimation.
Then, we translate this information through robot pro-
gramming.
• We propose a arm movement dataset based on the the-
ory of the kinesphere [8] using Unity3D 2 and different
person models extracted from Mixamo 3.
• We build a test dataset which consists videos of 10 test
subjects performing 10 preset movements to be used in
experiments.
.

II. R ELATED WORKS
Mini-introduction of the section
  
The ﬁeld of Robot Programming by Demonstration has been
around for a few years as people wanted to discover new
ways to teach a robot movements without the need for
expensive equipment like detectors. Thus, a great variety
of methods related to this ﬁeld were proposed over the
years to improve the results more and more. In this section,
related works that use techniques similar to the ones we use
are mentioned. These articles mention the disadvantages of
relying on kinesthetic demonstration when you want to teach
a robot such as the fact that robots can only learn movements
for speciﬁc cases and situations, which causes them to make
mistakes before various required changes in movement.
First Related Work
  
In [1], the authors propose a new LfD (Learning from
Demonstration) system that learns to perform the actions
demonstrated through the recognition of patterns and objects
in an experimental environment. They use the YOLO and
OpenPose algorithms to obtain the action that is performed
and smooth the results with a Gaussian ﬁlter, without using
or estimating the human pose. Instead, we estimate the
human pose and then use a neural network to convert human
arm movements into robotic arm movements that represent
the same action.
Second Related Work
  
In [9], the authors propose an action segmentation approach
by frame and recognition that is invariant to the number or
order of the object instances, does not require a temporal
prioritization of the segmentation and predicts actions for
each hand individually through the relationship between
the object and the action. During the 2D pre-processing
phase, object detection is done with the YOLO algorithm
and the user’s hands are detected using OpenPose. These
objects are later used to locate the human pose in 3D space.
Instead, we estimate the 2D human pose directly and then
use a 3D lifting method to convert the previously calculated
coordinates from a 2D plane to a 3D plane. Also, we use
this method since it allows us to improve the prediction of
the network for the ﬁnal position.
Third Related Work
  
In [10], the authors construct a robotic arm training model
using videos of workers performing designated input tasks.
The method used to solve the problem is visual demonstra-
tion by creating a context translation method based on coded
networks. A Markov network and a convolutional network
are built to be able to translate human movements to the
movements of a robot, generating a cycle of iterations in
which the movement is perfected until the task is perfectly
achieved. In other words, they analyze the context (setting,
orientation, etc.) to decide how the robot will move. Instead,
we seek to imitate human movements without considering
external factors to streamline the process of Robot Program-
ming by Demonstration.
Fourth Related Work
  
In [11], the authors propose a method that learns the
movements in a kinesthetic way and receives visual support
to know if there are alterations in the environment. This
allows the robots to make movements in the face of possible
changes in the environment. Instead, we use a visual aid to
estimate the pose and later be able to teach the robot that
movement; however, we do not analyze the possible changes
in the environment, since this process would considerably
increase the complexity and make the process relatively
slower, which generates an unnecessary waste of time in
cases where there are no changes in the environment.

Mini-introduction of the section
  
In this section, the main concepts used in our work are
presented. We aim to teach movements to a robot, using
computer vision methods such as pose estimation to extract
information from videos of a user’s body.
First Subject
  
Deﬁnition 1 (Pose estimation [13]): It is the retrieval of
geometric information regarding human movement from 2D
images.
When only one point of view is available (monocular cam-
era), the appearance or arrangement of the points of interest
of objects tends to be more difﬁcult to deduce.
Deﬁnition 2 (2D Pose estimation [14]): 2D pose esti-
mation tries to extract the human posture data in a two-
dimensional environment that is used for training and cor-
rection purposes.
Example 1 (2D Pose Estimation): We can see the 2D pose
estimation with the Open Pose algorithm of a soccer player
kicking a ball in the Figure 1a.
Deﬁnition 3 (3D Pose estimation [14]): 3D pose esti-
mation tries to estimate the posture obtained in a three-
dimensional simulated environment to be used in real con-
texts such as robot training and software simulations.
Example 2 (3D Pose Estimation): We can see the 3D pose
estimation of a man stretching his arms to grab a box in the
Figure 1b.
Estimating the pose of an image or video held by several
people simultaneously is much more complex than estimat-
ing the pose of a single person, since the process is affected
by a greater number of factors.
.

ming is the speciﬁcation of the desired motions of the robot
such that it may perform sequences of prestored motions or
motions computed as functions of sensory input.
The problem is the complexity of robot programming re-
mains one of the major barriers in adopting robotic technol-
ogy for “Small and Medium-sized Enterprises” (SMEs).

B. Method
Mini-introduction of the section
  
In this section, the main contributions proposed in this
project will be detailed.
1) Architecture: .
First Contribution
  
The main contribution of this research consists mainly in the
use of computer vision and deep learning techniques to be
able to correctly and accurately estimate the human poses
captured in each frame of a video and optimize their saving
in a database to be able to reproduce them in a robot.
In the Figure 3, we can see the main process begins with a
video in which a person moves their arms.
• Upload Video: From the application a video is up-
loaded4, which can be a video recorded with a camera
connected to the computer or a video in “.mp4” format
that is inside the computer.
• Frame separation: Once the video is loaded, it is sepa-
rated into frames.
• 2D Pose Estimation: The OpenPose neural network
predicts a series of 2D conﬁdence maps to estimate
the possible locations of the body parts and establishes
a series of vectors related to the afﬁnities between the
body parts. Afterwards, a greedy approach is performed
to infer the key points of the human body in the
image and transform it into two arrays that contain the
information of the human skeleton.
• 3D Pose Estimation: The human skeleton predicted is
used as input for the 3D lifting algorithm that raises
the coordinates of the 2D reference points to 3D and
projects it into a valid 3D pose space. When the 3D
points are obtained we only save the points of the arms:
the shoulder, elbow and hand.
• Prediction of movements to be executed by the robot:
The most complex part when transferring the move-
ments of a person to a robot is to classify their move-
ments so that the robot can be instructed what to do
in each case. Therefore, a generalization of movements
is proposed that simpliﬁes the possible movements of
an arm in a set of combinations that the robot can
better understand. The structure of these combinations
will be detailed in the construction of the dataset (see
Section III-B2). Once the possible movements have
been deﬁned, it is necessary to implement a classiﬁ-
cation method that translates them into the robot. We
previously trained two neural network that predicts a
general pose of the human arm based on the directions
of the kinesphere. We use these neural network to have
a better result in the prediction of the human arm. The
output of the neural network is a label of the direction
of the arm. With the predicted directions we obtain a
point that will be used to simulate the human movement
in the robot arm.
• Robot simulation: It should be clariﬁed that the robotic
arm simulator that we use is not capable of replicat-
ing a movement, because it only has 3 articulations.
Therefore, it is necessary to ﬁnd a point of conversion
between human movements and those that the robot can
perform. With this process the robot can simulate the
arm movements.
Second Contribution
  
2) Arm Movement Dataset: The second contribution of
this work is a manually generated dataset that is used to
generalize the human movements predicted with the pose
estimation techniques. The dataset has 31 models performing
176 movements. The ﬁrst thing that was done is determine
what movements the human body can perform. For this, an
investigation was carried out to determine a way to establish
these movements. According to the theory of the kinesphere,
the directions in which the human body can extend can be
generalized.
Fig. 4: All combinations of directions of the elbow-hand vector
when the shoulder-elbow vector is ”0”.
The ﬁrst ones are the 6 one-dimensional directions: up,
down, right, left, forward and backward. Then follow the
two-dimensional directions that are formed by the union of
two planes: right up, left up, right down, left down, forward
up, forward down, back up, back down, diagonal right
forward, diagonal left forward, diagonal right back and left
diagonal behind. Finally, when using a third plane, 8 three-
dimensional directions can be recognized: right forward up,
left forward up, right back up, left back up, right forward
down, left forward down, right back down, left back down. In
this way, these 26 directions are deﬁned in which each vector
of the arm can move and the combinations of the two arm
vectors are made. The combinations that a person cannot
perform without forcing their body (e.g., see Figure 4) to
locate the most common directions are manually removed
and they are left with a list with the following format.
Subsequently, 3D models were searched in Mixamo, which
can graphically represent the movements of the previously
obtained list. After choosing 31 models, a project was created
in Unity3D where the combinations from the previous list
were manually programmed to generate a data set of images
labeled with the position they are mimicking. Finally, the
obtained 3D model dataset is used to train the neural network
mentioned at the beginning of this section.

In this subsection, the results obtained in the previous

Discussion
  
1) Validation with metrics: As mentioned above, the
purpose of the ADBP metric is to determine the value of the
distance between the predicted point and the actual point,
so the smaller the result, the better. Whereas, the other
metric aims to validate how many predictions were made
correctly, so the higher the result, the better. Based on this,
it can be clearly evidenced that there are movements and
conﬁgurations at the time of recording that can negatively
affect the result of the prediction. Despite this, in a greater
number of cases it can be seen that the robotic arm responds
in a good way when predicting people’s movements. In
Table 5a it can be seen that an average distance of 0.8743
was obtained, which shows that the distance between the
predicted point and the real point is relatively small, it
is even almost half of the threshold (1.5) that we use to
deﬁne whether a prediction was correct. The best predictions
were obtained in the seventh and third movement with a
value of 0.372 and 0.471 respectively; while, the second
movement caused the worst prediction with a value of 1,415.
In addition, in Table 5b it can be seen that 82.67% of the
predictions were made correctly.
2) Comparison of classiﬁcation methods: As we previ-
ously mentioned, one of the objectives of the investigation
is to use a classiﬁcation algorithm to be able to correctly
label each human movement and translate it to the robotic
arms.
This way, the results on the Table 6a are the comparison of
the different classiﬁcation methods used with the shoulder-
elbow vector. In other hand, the Table 6b is the comparison
of the elbow-hand vector classiﬁcation methods, this table
has better results than the previous mentioned because it
use the result of the other vector prediction as an additional
input. As a result, the Table 6b is more important to realize
the ﬁnal result of the project and is the one that has more
thought when choosing a classiﬁcation algorithm. As we can
see in Table 6a, the algorithm that achieved the best results
was Random Forest with 0.73 in precision, recall and F1
slightly exceeding the result of the neural network. However,
in Table 6b we can see that the results of the neural network
far exceed those of the other methods with an F1 of 0.84.
That’s why it was decided to work with neural networks for
the development of the project.

V. C ONCLUSION
First Conclusion
  
In this work we can ﬁnd that the generalization of the
arm movements can be effectively used to translate the
human motion to robotic assistants, as could be seen in the
different tests, the precision of the movement is strongly
related to the quality of the recording and the pose estimation
algorithm used. As we mentioned before is impossible to
replicate at one hundred percent the human movements
with all robotic assistants. They have different shapes and
purpose, and the limitations of their articulations make them
can’t move like a human does. However, as we see in the
experimentation you can deﬁne a translation condition that
make this assistant perform a similar movement that meets
the user’s requirements. This condition can be like keep the
center of the unit, keep the articulations position, keep the
position relative to the space or keep the hand point like our
research.
Second Conclusion
  
It is very important to mention that covering the prob-
lem with classiﬁcation algorithms and vectors has given
a positive result. The second experiment shows that the
neural networks works better with the vector data predictions
than other classiﬁcation methods, this is attributed to the
ﬂexibility that they have to work with different types of data.
Finally, the best way to work with human motions is using
unitary vectors that describes the articulation direction, this
method avoid the problems such as the length of the limbs
and the relative position they have in space, it also reduce
the input data and normalice the results.
Future works
  
We have two main perspectives for our work: i) A future
approach with this research could be to augment the body
parts to be analyzed to employ a humanoid robot, for
example the NAO robot. ii) Another approach could be to
improve the pose estimation to be able to estimate the pose
of the arms with incomplete body videos, where at least both
arms are seen, i.e., the other parts of the body may not be
seen by the angle of the camera or because it is covered by
some object. To these, the proposed metrics of ADBP and
Percentage of Correct Predictions can be applied to evaluate
the predictions, thus ensuring their quality.

7.2. Case study: discovering toxicophores
A major issue in chemoinformatics is to establish relationships between chem-
icals and their activity in (eco)toxicity. Chemical fragments6 which cause toxic-
ity are called toxicophores and their discovery is at the core of prediction models
in (eco)toxicity [6, 52]. The aim of this study, which is part of a larger research
collaboration with the CERMN Lab, is to investigate the use of skypatterns for
discovering toxicophores.

Experimental protocol. The dataset is collected from the ECB web site 7. For
each chemical, the chemists associate it with hazard statement codes (HSC) in
3 categories: H400 (very toxic, CL50≤ 1 mg/L), H401 (toxic, 1 mg/L < CL50
≤ 10 mg/L), and H402 (harmful, 10 mg/L < CL50≤ 100 mg/L). We focus on
the H400 and H402 classes. The dataset T consists of 567 chemicals (transac-
tions), 372 from the H400 class and 195 from the H402 class. The chemicals
are encoded using 1450 frequent closed subgraphs (items) previously extracted8
with a 1% relative frequency threshold. Therefore, the extracted skypatterns are
made of these frequent closed subgraphs corresponding to chemical fragments.
Discovering candidate toxicophores is linked to a classiﬁcation problem w.r.t
the HSC and thus we use the growth-rate as a contrast measure [13]. Indeed,
when a pattern has a frequency which signiﬁcantly increases from the H402 class
to the H400 class, then it stands a potential structural alert related to an excess
of the toxicity. If a chemical has, in its structure, fragments that are related to an
effect, then it is more likely to be toxic. Emerging patterns embody this natural
idea by using the growth-rate measure. On the other hand, real-world datasets
are often noisy and patterns with low frequency may be artefacts. Then we add
the frequency measure to ensure the representativeness of the patterns (i.e., the
higher the frequency, the better).
The skypattern framework enables to integrate measures coming from the
background domain. In ecotoxicity, chemists know that the aromaticity mea-
sure is a chemical property that favors toxicity since their metabolites can lead
to very reactive species which can interact with biomacromolecules in a harm-
ful way (the higher the aromaticity, the higher the toxicity hypothesis). The
chemical knowledge provides the aromaticity of the chemical fragments and we
compute the aromaticity of pattern as the mean of the aromaticity of its chemical
fragments.

2. Related work
2.1. Pattern mining
Frequent itemset mining was ﬁrst described in [10]. The problem can be
deﬁned as follows: a transaction is a subset of a given set of items I, and a
transaction database, denotedT , is a set of such transactions. A subset X ofI
is a frequent itemset inT if the number of transactions containing X exceeds a
given threshold, denoted by σ. One of the earliest ﬁndings in the data mining
community is that a mining process usually produces large collections of pat-
terns. Many researchers have proposed methods to reduce the size of the output.
The constraint-based pattern mining framework [30], the condensed representa-
tions [29] and the compression of the dataset by exploiting Minimum Descrip-
tion Length Principle [31], to name a few. A general observation is that patterns
represent “fragmented knowledge”, and often there is no clear view of how these
knowledge fragments interact and combine to produce a global model. Recent
approaches have therefore used schemes such as pattern teams [32], constraint-
based pattern set mining [33] and pattern selections [34] that aim to minimize
the redundancy and the number of patterns. A common theme in these studies
is to select patterns from the initial large set of patterns on the basis of their
usefulness in a given context. This approach comes within the general trend to
produce pattern sets i.e. sets of patterns satisfying properties on the whole set
of patterns [33]. Other approaches take advantage of closed patterns to maxi-
mize a speciﬁc measure such as the growth rate for emerging patterns [35] and
the area for tiling [36, 14]. Often, these methods focus on optimizing a global
measure on the discovered pattern set and neglect the relationships between pat-
terns. Moreover, these approaches suffer from a lack of ﬂexibility to express
the queries requested by the analyst. For each method, the user has to under-
stand its semantics and express queries satisfying its algorithmic properties and
constraints.
Another class of techniques considers statistical signiﬁcance of patterns. The
objective is to extract patterns for which a given characteristic (usually the fre-
quency) deviates so much from its expected value under a null model that it is
unlikely to have been generated by it. The frequency of a pattern is then con-
sidered as a random variable, whose distribution under the null hypothesis has
to be calculated or approximated, and the signiﬁcance of the pattern is assessed
through a statistical test that compares the expected frequency under the null
model to the observed frequency.
5
The null model will ideally involve a trade-off between adjustment to the data
and simplicity. The model should capture some characteristics of the data, other-
wise all patterns would be signiﬁcant, but it should not overﬁt the data. A num-
ber of works have explored various notions of statisticalsigniﬁcance for itemsets
and have proposed novel and efﬁcient methods for their extraction. In [37], an
approach is developed to mine statistically signiﬁcant association rules, under
an assumption of independence between the condition and the consequence of
the rule. The expected frequency for the condition and consequence is derived
analytically, based on the marginal frequencies of the dataset. In [20], the au-
thors use two null models to calculate the expected frequencies. The ﬁrst is an
independence model, where items are assumed to be mutually independent and
their probabilities of occurrence are their observed marginal relative frequencies.
The second model is obtained by assuming a distinct distribution for each trans-
action, where each of the items has the same transaction-dependent probability
of occurrence. This second model considers large transactions to be less inter-
esting, because two such transactions are likely to share items by chance. As
the expected frequency is anti-monotone (the expected frequency of an itemset
is greater than the expected frequencies of its subsets), the authors only con-
sider closed itemsets (itemsets having no super-set with the same observed fre-
quency). They then introduce the MINI algorithm to mine for non-redundant
unexpected itemsets, by penalizing itemsets overlapping with itemsets with the
highest p-values. An alternative to the independence model is the maximum en-
tropy model [38]. A related framework is developed in [39], where unexpected
itemsets with respect to a maximum entropy model are used to update the model
iteratively, resulting in a large coverage of the database and minimal redundancy
between the itemsets.
Pattern mining and Constraint programming. Pattern mining beneﬁt from
the recent progress on cross-fertilization between data mining and Constraint
Programming (CP) [40, 41, 42, 25]. CP is a general declarative methodology
for solving constraint satisfaction problems. Within this framework, the user
speciﬁes in a declarative way by using constraints what the problem is rather
than a method dedicated to the problem. Then a general solver provides the
complete set of solutions satisfying all the constraints. Results show that these
methods are more expressive and work well.
6
2.2. Skyline
The notion of dominance is at the core of the skyline processing. This notion
can also be used to handle many pattern mining problems that involved pairwise
comparisons between patterns [3]. The skyline points can be viewed as compro-
mise points with respect to a given set of criteria. Skyline computation is strongly
related to mathematical and microeconomics problems such as maximum vec-
tors [43], Pareto set [44], and multi-objective optimization [45]. Since its redis-
covery within the database community by B ¨orzs¨onyi et al. [28], many methods
have been developed for answering skyline queries that can handle various con-
straints in different computational environments [46, 47]. Skyline queries focus
on the extraction of tuples from a given dataset and assume that all the elements
are in the dataset, while the skypattern mining task consists of extracting patterns
which are elements of the frontier deﬁned by the given measures. The skypat-
tern problem is clearly harder because the search space for skypatterns is much
larger than the search space for skylines (cf. Section 3.2). Another aspect of
preference-based processing is the top-k procedure [26, 27]. A ranking function
fr is applied to patterns, and the k best patterns with the highest score with re-
spect to fr are returned. As previously mentioned, this approach suffers from
some limitations. The choice of k is not trivial (i.e., the horizon problem). A
low value may miss useful patterns and too high a value introduces redundancy
within the produced patterns (i.e., highly similar patterns). This limitation is the
main motivation for the notion of the “most informative patterns” (MIP) that
were proposed in [48]. MIPs can be seen as patterns that locally dominate other
patterns according to a scoring function. This approach shares a similar spirit to
ours as it also limits the number of enumerated patterns to a more manageable
level. However, in contrast to our approach, work on MIPs includes a notion of
dominance that is local and speciﬁc only to subsets of patterns.

3. Problem Statement and Overview
We introduce in this section some basic deﬁnitions and the formal problem
statement. We also give an overview of the two methods (called Aetheris and
CP+Sky) that we propose. These methods fully exploit an adequate representa-
tion of patterns dedicated to user preferences [49]. Our study is interesting for
several reasons. By carefully selecting patterns that are “the best available” for
a given set of preferences, we reduce signiﬁcantly the output and we limit the
curse of “pattern explosion”. The user is guaranteed that only the most signif-
icant patterns w.r.t. his criteria are present in the ﬁnal result. Last but not least,
our approach is threshold-free. Thresholds are entirely optional, depending on
the analyst’s needs and do not depend on the algorithm. Only the preferences
and the dataset are required as an input.

This large variety of measures can be source of preferences for the end-user

Deﬁnition 1 ((Pareto-)Dominance). Given a set of measures M⊆M , a pat-
ternX dominates another pattern Y with respect to M, denoted by X≻M Y ,
iff for any measurem∈ M,m(X)≥ m(Y ) and there existsm∈ M such that
m(X)> m(Y ). Two patternsX andY are said to be indistinct with respect to

M =∅, thenX =∅ Y ). Finally,X⪰M Y denotes that (X≻M Y )∨(X =M Y ).
Notice that we deﬁne the Pareto dominance only with symbol greater than
(i.e., >) assuming that the end-user wants to maximize a set of measures. If
she aims to minimize a measure m, without loss of generality, it is equivalent
to maximize the measure m′ =−m. For this reason, our deﬁnition of p-value
contains a minus.

3.2. The skypattern mining problem
Given a set of measures M, if a pattern is dominated by another according
to all measures of M, it is irrelevant and must be discarded in the output. The
notion of skyline pattern, skypattern for short, formalizes this intuition.
Deﬁnition 2 (Skypattern operator). Given a pattern set P ⊆L and a set of
measuresM⊆M , a skypattern of P with respect to M is a pattern not dom-
inated in P with respect to M. The skypattern operator Sky(P,M ) returns all
the skypatterns ofP with respect toM:
Sky(P,M ) ={X∈P|̸∃ Y∈P :Y≻M X}
Then, the skypattern problem can be stated:
Problem 1. Given a set of measures M⊆M , the skypattern mining problem
is to evaluate the querySky(L,M ).

In the general case, the skypattern mining problem is challenging because of the
very high number of candidate patterns (i.e |L|). Indeed, a naive enumeration
ofL is not feasible. For example, with 1000 items a naive approach will need
to compute (21000− 1)×|M| measures and then compare them. A less naive
approach based on heuristics (such as the anti-monotonicity of some measures)
may give some results. However, the performance will be closely tied to the
underlying properties of the data sets. For instance, in the case of the frequency
measure, the density of the data set plays a major role in the performance and
some algorithms are not able to extract frequent patterns at very low thresholds.
Nevertheless, considering the following property sheds new insights into an ef-
ﬁcient computation of skypattern queries.

includesSky(L,M )) by means of 1) pattern condensed representations and 2)

Pattern condensed representations. In many pattern mining tasks (e.g., associ-
ation rule mining or clustering), condensed representations signiﬁcantly reduce
the mining effort without loss of precision. Could we use this principle in the
case of skypattern mining? A direct approach would be to compute a concise
representation for each measure m∈ M, but this is generally not possible be-
cause some measures, such as area or length, are simply not condensable (i.e.,
the condensed representation is equal to |L|). Therefore, our problem can be
reformulated as following: given a set of measuresM, how to identify a smaller
set of measuresM′ which allows the computation of a concise representation on
the patterns without loss of skypatterns? In addition, how to use this set of mea-
sures to extract efﬁciently the skypatterns without redundancies? We address
this problem in the next sections.
Dynamic pruning. Instead of extracting the whole condensed representation and
then, applying theSky operator, this operator may be applied locally during the
extraction. Indeed, as soon as a pattern is a candidate skypattern, the search space
dominated by this pattern can be directly eliminated. In other words, each new
candidate skypattern adds a constraint allowing to safely prune the search space.
These new constraints avoid to consider patterns that should be extracted by
considering the technique indicated above ant only based on pattern condensed
representation. But, how to manage these new constraints that can be obtained
during the extraction to dynamically reduce the search space? We will see in
Section 6 that CSP enable us to provide a positive answer.

4. Skylineability
The set of skypatterns has no good property like downard closure or convex-
ity. Conventional techniques used for pruning the pattern search space like anti-
monotone properties are thus ineffective. However, the computation of skypat-
terns is right as soon as the Sky operator is applied on a collection S that is a
superset of skypatterns. Of course the smaller this collection is, the faster the
computation. Basically the skylineability aims at computing a reduced collec-
tion of representative patterns of the skypatterns (cf. the end of the previous
section) through the notion of free [18], and closed patterns [16].
4.1. Skylineability of a set of measures
Intuitively, the skylineability points out local extrema in the search space.
Only these extrema may therefore be skypatterns. Thus it is not necessary to
take into account the other patterns which are necessarily dominated by these
extrema. Assume that two patterns X andY such that X⊂ Y have the same
value for each measure ofM′:X =M ′ Y . IfY always dominatesX with respect
toM, thenM is said to be maximallyM′-skylineable. This information is great
because it allows us not to consider non-maximal patterns (here, X) which are
dominated by maximal patterns (here,Y ).
For instance, let us consider patterns from T that maximize the frequency
and the area: M ={freq,area}. In our example (cf. Table 1), the patterns B
andAB have the same frequencyB =freq AB andAB≻M B because the area
ofAB is greater than that of B. More generally, two patterns X⊂Y such that
X =freq Y implies that Y ≻M X. Such set of measures M ={freq,area} is
said to be maximally{freq}-skylineable. In practice, local extrema are not nec-
essarily the longest patterns (closed patterns/maximal patterns of equivalence
classes) and the concept of skylineability is dually deﬁned with the shortest pat-
terns (i.e., free patterns/minimal patterns of equivalence classes):
Deﬁnition 3 (Minimal skylineability). Given a set of measures M′ ⊆ M, a
set of measures M is said to be (strictly) minimally M′-skylineable iff for any
patternsX =M ′ Y such thatX⊂Y , one hasX⪰M Y (respectivelyX≻M Y ).
Deﬁnition 4 (Maximal skylineability). Given a set of measures M′⊆M , a
set of measures M is said to be (strictly) maximally M′-skylineable iff for any
patternsX =M ′ Y such thatX⊂Y , one hasY⪰M X (respectivelyY≻M X).

From the previous deﬁnitions, given a set of measures M which is maximally
M′-skylineable, ifX =M ′ Y andX⊃Y , it is clear thatX cannot be dominated
by Y on M. For instance, M = {freq,area} is strictly maximally {freq}-
skylineable becausearea(X) strictly increases with the cardinality of X (when
the frequency remains constant). Notice that {freq} is (weakly) maximally (or
minimally){freq}-skylineable and that {length(X)} is strictly maximally ∅-
skylineable.
Property 2. Given a set of measuresM, there is at least one setM′⊆M such
thatM is minimally and maximallyM′-skylineable.
Property 2 is a very important result as it means that a set of measures is
always skylineable (due to the fact thatM is at leastM-skylineable). Obviously,
for a set of measuresM, the smaller1M′, the stronger itsM′-skylineability. For
instance,{freq}-skylineability is more interesting than{freq,area}-skylineability
becausearea is not a condensable function [49]: there is no pair of distinct pat-
ternsX andY such thatX ={freq,area} Y . How to choose automatically a subset
M′ is discussed next.

6. Mining skypatterns using Dynamic CSP
This section describes our dynamic method called CP+Sky for the skypattern
mining problem. We show how this problem can be modeled and solved by
using Dynamic CSP [51]. A major advantage of this method is to improve the
mining step during the process thanks to constraints dynamically posted and
stemming from the current set of the candidate skypatterns. These constraints
avoid producing solutions dominated by the current solutions and thus reduce the
search space. More precisely, each time a solution is found, a new constraint is
dynamically posted. The process stops when we cannot enlarge the “dominated
area” (cf. Figure 1). The set of obtained representative patterns is a subset
of the set of representative patterns extracted with Aetheris (cf. Section 3.3).
Finally, the completeness of our method is insured by the completeness of the
CSP solver. The implementation has been carried out inGecode2 extending the
(CSP based) pattern extractor developed by [42].

1. Introduction
The process of extracting useful patterns from data, called pattern mining, is an
important subﬁeld of data mining, and has been used in a wide range of applica-
tions and domains such as bioinformatics [5], chemoinformatics [6], social net-
work analysis [7], web mining [8] and network intrusions detection [9]. Since the
seminal papers of Agrawal et al. [10], Mannila et al. [11], a considerable num-
ber of patterns, such as itemsets, strings, sequences, trees and graphs, have been
studied and used in real-world applications. Nowadays, many pattern extraction
problems like subgroup discovery [12], discriminant pattern mining [13], and
tiling [14] are understood from both theoretical and computational perspectives.
Most existing pattern mining approaches enumerate patterns with respect to
a given set of constraints that range from simple to complex. For instance, given
a transaction database, a well-known pattern mining task is to enumerate all
itemsets (i.e., sets of items) that appear in at least s transactions. Another min-
ing approach is to extract from a given graph database all subgraphs that have
a diameter larger than l, connectivity higher than c, and where each vertex has
a degree bounded by d. The output for these mining approaches, however, can
be extremely large even for moderately sized datasets. For instance, in the worst
case, the number of frequent itemsets is exponential in the size of the items in
the dataset. So far, the community has made great efforts on sophisticated algo-
rithms which push the constraints deep into the mining process [15]. But also
in condensation (i.e., reduction) techniques to limit the number of output pat-
terns depending on the application contexts. Examples of these condensation
techniques include: closed itemsets [16], non-derivable itemsets [17] and free
itemsets [18]. Several other studies in the pattern mining community focused on
approaches that consider a statistical null hypothesis and which assess patterns
according to how much their observed frequency (or more rarely the length [19])
deviates from their expected frequency under the null model [20, 21, 22, 23].
One of the key points of such approaches is to choose an appropriate null hy-
pothesis. It will involve a trade-off between adjustment to the data and simplic-
ity.
2
The pattern mining community, however, has paid less attention to combining
mining constraints. In practice, many constraints entail choosing threshold val-
ues such as the well-used minimal frequency. This notion of“thresholding” has
serious drawbacks. Unless speciﬁc domain knowledge is available, the choice is
often arbitrary and may lead to a very large number of extracted patterns which
can reduce the success of any subsequent data analysis. This drawback is even
deeper when several thresholds have to be combined. A second drawback is
the stringent enumeration aspect : a pattern is either above or below a thresh-
old. But what about patterns that respect only some thresholds? Should they
be discarded? It is often very difﬁcult to apply subtle selection mechanisms.
There are very few works such as [24, 25] which propose to introduce a softness
criterion into the mining process. Other studies blend user preferences in the
mining task in order to limit the number of extracted patterns such as the top-k
patterns [26, 27]. By associating each pattern with a rank score, this approach
returns an ordered list of the k patterns with the highest score to the user. How-
ever, combining several measures in a single scoring function is difﬁcult and the
performance of top-k approaches are often sensitive to the size of the datasets
and to the threshold value,k.
Here, we present a new framework that aims to make the results of pattern
mining useful from a user-preference point of view . To this end, we integrate
into the pattern discovery process the idea of skyline queries [28] in order to
mine skyline patterns in a threshold-free manner. Such queries have attracted
considerable attention due to their importance in multi-criteria decision making
and economics where they are usually called “Pareto efﬁciency or optimality
queries”. Brieﬂy, in a multidimensional space where a preference is deﬁned for
each dimension, a point a dominates another point b if a is better (i.e., more
preferred) than b in at least one dimension, and a is not worse than b on every
other dimension. For example, a user selecting a set of patterns may prefer a
pattern with a low frequency, a large length and a high conﬁdence. In this case,
we say that patterna dominates another patternb ifa.frequency≤b.frequency ,
a.length≥ b.length,a.confidence≥ b.confidence, where at least one strict in-
equality holds. Given a set of patterns, the skyline set contains the patterns that
are not dominated by any other patterns.
3
Skyline pattern mining is interesting for several reasons. Firstly, skyline process-
ing does not require any threshold selection or ranking function. Secondly, the
formal property of dominance satisﬁed by the skyline pattern deﬁnes a global
interest with semantics easily understood by the user. These semantics are dis-
cussed at length in the economics literature, where the Pareto efﬁciency is ap-
plied to the selection of alternatives in resource distributions. However, while
this notion of skylines has been extensively developed in engineering and database
applications, it has remained unused for data mining purposes except for just a
few works in the last three years [1, 2, 3, 4].

Contributions and roadmap
Here, we present signiﬁcant extensions of our recent papers [1, 4] on the dis-
covery of skyline patterns, or “skypatterns”. First, we detail the static method
based on the theoretical relationships with pattern condensed representations
(representations which return a subset of the patterns having the same expres-
siveness as the whole set of patterns [29]). Second, we describe the dynamic
method which produces a continuous reﬁnement of the skyline constraints based
on the extracted patterns. This is achieved through a Dynamic CSP (Constraint
Satisfaction Problems) framework. Third, the key notion of “skylineability”
which constitutes the cornerstone of our two methods is explained in more de-
tail. Furthermore, we discuss the generic aspect of the skypattern framework
and we show that we can combine different measures to provide statistical as-
sessments of pattern mining results. Finally, we present an extensive empirical
study which includes a wide range of datasets and comparisons of our tech-
niques. This enables us to draw some lessons on the strengths and weaknesses
of each method.
The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys the works re-
lated to skyline pattern analysis. Section 3 introduces some basic deﬁnitions,
the formal problem statement and an overview of our work. The key notion of
skylineability is then studied in Section 4. Section 5 discusses the computation
of pattern condensed representation for skypattern queries. Section 6 discusses
skylineability but within a dynamic CSP framework. We report an empirical
study on several datasets and a case study from the chemoinformatics domain in
Section 7. Finally, Section 8 discusses the learnt lessons.

8. Conclusion and Perspectives
In this paper, we investigate in detail the skyline pattern mining problem by
studying the theoretical relationships between pattern condensed representations
and skypatterns. Based on the concept of skylineability, we have devised the
static method Aetheris and the dynamic method CP+Sky. Aetheris exploits the
pattern condensed representations to provide a proper superset of skypatterns
to apply the Sky operator. CP+Sky iteratively reﬁnes the skyline constraints
using the extracted patterns. This gives a better pruning of the search space. We
produce the complete set of skypatterns in a generic way (i.e., with a large set of
measures that includes statistical assessments such as the p-value). The practical
goal is to make the result of pattern mining useful from auser-preference point of
view. One strength of the approach lies in the fact that no threshold has to be set,
the end-user only needs to specify as input the dataset and the set of measures
she is interested in.
An extensive empirical study as well as a case study from chemoinformat-
ics show the efﬁciency of our two algorithms according to both quantitative and
qualitative aspects. Despite the gain in generality brought by the CSP frame-
work and the fact that Aetheris takes beneﬁt of the pruning strategies based on
the anti-monotonicity to extract patterns, CP+Sky competes with Aetheris and
even outperforms it in some cases. Moreover, with CP+Sky, the search order of
the patterns may signiﬁcantly impact the efﬁciency of the CSP machinery. Inves-
tigating the most beneﬁcial order in which patterns are produced is a promising
research track to maximize the effectiveness of the strategy of the constraints
dynamically posted.
Skypattern mining can generally be applied to a wide range of problems
by adapting either the language or the dominance relation. For instance, the
language of sequences or graphs can also produce skypatterns. The dominance
relation can be changed or extended to take into account different other criteria
for user-preferences. We sketch now these two issues.
Language. Our formalization, and especially the skylineability notion, is gen-
eral enough to be applied to a large set of languages (sequences, trees and graph
for instance). However, a change in the language can impact the efﬁciency of
the extraction methods. Regarding Aetheris, the efﬁciency of the approach is
based on Theorem 1 involving the distinct and indistinct operators. As afore-
mentioned, with itemset patterns and the frequency measure, the distinct oper-
ator corresponds to the well-known closed or free frequent pattern condensed
representations. Consequently, the efﬁcient extraction of skypatterns for more
complex languages (i.e., skyline sequential patterns or skyline graph patterns) is
strongly correlated to the advances and progress on complex pattern condensed
representations.
36
Evaluating efﬁciently the distinct operator on more complex patterns such as
sequences, trees and graphs implies additional challenges. To cite one, in the
case of sequences, convenient properties such as the free patterns apriori prop-
erty [56], which implies effective search space pruning, cannot be used. How-
ever, there are already many methods of extraction dedicated to closed sequen-
tial patterns, closed graphs and so on. Extending CP+Sky to other languages is a
challenging task due to the difﬁculty of modeling complex patterns. To the best
of our knowledge, only certain types of pattern in sequence mining have been
successfully studied [57, 58].
Dominance relation. Dominance relation is based on two levels: the measures
that form basic preference criteria (e.g., frequency, area) and the combination
of these basic preferences (here, the Pareto composition). Primitive-based mea-
sures are ﬂexible enough to allow the user to express a wide variety of criteria.
Indeed, classical interestingness measures for pattern mining (such as frequency,
growth rate), utility functions and the most recent measures of signiﬁcance (like
p-value) fall within this framework. Of course, Aetheris depends again on con-
densed representations that are well-adapted for the desired measures. Con-
versely, all interestingness measures are easily expressible with CP+Sky.
Thanks to its declarative nature, CSP offers through CP+Sky a very ﬂexible
way to change the dominance relationship. For instance the strict dominance
(i.e., a pattern is dominated by another pattern when the latter has a better value
for all measures inM), is easily conﬁgurable with CSP and this relaxation of the
dominance relation leads to the mining of soft-skypatterns [4].
